## 1. "First water test: ammonia 0.25 ppm, what now?"

Welcome, and good that you tested. A reading above 0 for ammonia means the filter bacteria are not yet keeping up with the waste. Ammonia is toxic to fish at any visible level, so the usual advice is: do a partial water change (around 25-30%) with dechlorinated water that matches the tank temperature, then retest the next day and keep changing water until it reads 0. Look for the cause: overfeeding, a dead fish or plant rot, or a filter that was cleaned in tap water. If the tank is brand new and has no fish, tell us, because that is a different situation (you may be mid-cycle).

## 2. "Nitrite is 0.5 in a tank with fish, help"

Nitrite is toxic, and a reading above 0 means the second group of bacteria is behind the first, which is common after adding fish or disturbing the filter. Test ammonia as well. Many people do water changes until nitrite reads 0 and retest daily. Some kit makers instead suggest a bacteria product at low readings (for example NT Labs' pack text at 0.25-0.5 ppm, https://thefishbarn.co.uk/product/nt-labs-nitrite-test/, read 2026-10-24); different sources suggest different first steps, so ask what your kit's instructions say too. Feed lightly meanwhile.

## 3. "Nitrates keep creeping up, how often should I change water?"

Nitrate is where the cycle ends up, so some is normal. Under about 20 ppm is generally comfortable, and 40 or more is a sign to act. What matters most is the rate: if it rises about 10 ppm a week, a weekly partial change keeps it flat; if it jumps faster, check feeding amount, stocking and filter cleanliness. Live plants help use it up. Test before and after a change for a few weeks to learn your own tank's rate.

## 4. "KH is low (3-4), is that a problem?"

KH is the buffer that stops pH from swinging. Cycling bacteria use it up daily, and a thin buffer can let pH drop suddenly instead of gradually, which stresses fish and slows the bacteria. Low single digits is worth acting on: regular partial water changes with tap water that has decent KH are the simplest supply line, and test weekly rather than monthly until it is steady. Check your tap water's KH first; if it is low at the tap, a buffering product is the next step, added slowly.

## 5. "Going away for a week, what do I tell the fish sitter?"

Keep it boring and written down: one page with what to feed, how much, and when; where the food is; one line saying "nothing else, no extra feeding"; who to call if the tank looks wrong (cloudy, fish at the surface, a dead fish); and a request for a photo each day or two. Pre-portion food in labelled pots or a pill organiser so over-feeding is not possible, and set the lights on a timer. Do a water change a day or two before you leave, not on the day.
